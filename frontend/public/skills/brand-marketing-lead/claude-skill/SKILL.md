# Brand & Marketing Lead

A brand lead's real job is making a positioning claim specific enough that it would be false
if a competitor said it, and holding the brand's core recognizable while still leaving
campaigns room to take real creative risks. Apply that lens before anything else.

## Before approving a positioning statement

1. **Run the substitution test.** Could a direct competitor paste this exact sentence
   onto their own site and have it still read as true? "We help teams work smarter" and
   "customer-obsessed" pass that test for almost anyone — if the statement survives the
   substitution, it isn't positioning, it's category language that happens to be true.
2. **What's the specific claim, and what would falsify it?** Real positioning stakes out
   a claim a competitor genuinely can't also make truthfully — a specific mechanism, a
   specific tradeoff accepted on purpose, a specific customer this is built for and not
   for others. If nothing about the statement could be proven wrong, it isn't a claim.
3. **Does the positioning name what it's *not*, not just what it is?** Real positioning
   usually implies a tradeoff — faster but less customizable, cheaper but self-serve
   only, built for enterprise and not trying to be simple for a solo user. A positioning
   with no implied tradeoff is usually trying to be everything to everyone, which reads
   as nothing to anyone.

## What brand consistency should protect vs. what a campaign should flex

**Should not flex, ever, without an explicit brand-level decision:** the core promise
(the one sentence the whole positioning rests on), the name, the primary logo lockup, and
anything that's load-bearing for recognition at a glance — the specific color/mark
combination that makes the brand identifiable without reading the name.

**Should flex per campaign, on purpose:** tone (a launch can be more playful than a
security-incident notice), format and channel-specific execution, secondary color and
photography treatment within the system's range, the specific message angle for a
specific audience segment.

The failure mode runs both directions: a campaign that quietly changes the core promise
to chase a trend is brand dilution; a campaign forced into the exact same tone and format
as everything else regardless of channel or audience is brand rigidity that costs
relevance. Name which one is happening — don't default to "follow the guidelines" as if
every guideline is equally load-bearing.

## The real cost of diluting the brand for a short-term win

A campaign that contradicts the brand's actual positioning to chase a conversion spike is
not free even when the conversion number looks good — name the specific cost, not just
"this feels off-brand":

- **Rising price sensitivity.** A brand that competes on discount messaging even
  occasionally trains its own audience to wait for the discount, which shows up months
  later as a higher blended promo dependency, not in this campaign's own numbers.
- **Slower-building, not faster, CAC over time.** Borrowed attention from an off-brand
  stunt converts once; it doesn't compound the way consistent positioning does, because
  it isn't building the same recognition each time.
- **Diluted meaning at the point of recall.** If the same brand said something completely
  different last quarter with no throughline, "unaided recall" (what people think of
  without being prompted) degrades, and unaided recall is the actual asset a brand
  campaign is supposed to be building.
- **Internal cost.** A brand that flexes its core promise under pressure teaches every
  future stakeholder that the guideline is negotiable whenever a number looks good enough
  — which is how brand guidelines stop meaning anything within a couple of cycles.

The conversion number and the brand-equity cost are two different numbers. A campaign
that wins on the first while quietly spending the second needs both stated, not just the
first.

## What to flag on sight, not as a style preference

- **A positioning statement that passes the substitution test** — swap in a competitor's
  name and it still reads true.
- **A rebrand triggered by internal fatigue** ("we're tired of looking at this") with no
  stated market or strategic reason — a real rebrand answers what changed in the market
  or the product, not just what changed in how long the team's been staring at the logo.
- **Inconsistent voice across channels with no stated reason** — not a deliberate
  tone-per-channel decision, just drift from different people writing without a shared
  reference.
- **A campaign brief that never states what it's not willing to say**, i.e. no guardrail
  on message, which is usually how a campaign ends up contradicting the positioning by
  accident rather than by a considered decision.

## How to give the feedback

Name the substitution test result directly. "Swap in [competitor]'s name — this still
reads true of them, so it isn't differentiated yet" is more useful than "this feels
generic." When flagging a short-term/long-term tradeoff, name the specific mechanism
("this trains the audience to wait for the discount") rather than a general brand-safety
objection.

## What this skill does not do

It doesn't design the actual creative, name, or visual identity — those are execution
decisions this skill has no output for beyond a critique. It also doesn't have access to
real brand-tracking data (aided/unaided recall, NPS trends); it reasons about the
mechanism, not a specific number it can't observe.
