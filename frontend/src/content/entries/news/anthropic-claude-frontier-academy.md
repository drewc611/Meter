Anthropic launched Claude Frontier Academy on October 2, backed by a $100 million commitment to train 10,000 "Frontier Deployed Engineers" (FDEs) by the end of 2027. The program is explicitly structured like medical training: Steve Corfield, Anthropic's Global Head of Business Development and Partnerships, frames it as teaching people "the way our own engineers learn." Candidates start with a multi-day in-person course taught by Anthropic engineers and licensed instructors, work through a simulated enterprise deployment end to end -- choosing the right use case, clearing a security review, handing it off -- and finish with a graded practical on a new scenario. Those who pass earn a "Claude Resident Engineer" badge and move into a 12-week residency, leading a real Claude deployment inside their own employer with Anthropic engineers supporting them.

## Eight firms, eight similar complaints about talent

The first cohorts draw engineers from Accenture, Bain, Capgemini, Commonwealth Bank of Australia, Deloitte, McKinsey, Morgan Stanley and Novo Nordisk, training out of San Francisco, New York and London. What's notable is how consistent the partner quotes are on what the gap actually is. Dan Tinkoff, McKinsey's Senior Partner and Global Co-leader of QuantumBlack, wants the residency to extend his "technologists' toolkit" for client work; Faruk Muratovic, a Deloitte principal, calls it a way to "scale a discipline we've already tested in the field"; Geoffroy Pajot of Capgemini draws the sharpest line: "What I'm hearing from clients isn't about certification counts or training volume; it's about knowing firsthand how to solve real-world problems." None of the eight partner quotes mentions wanting more people who've passed a test -- all of them describe wanting people who've actually done the work once already.

## A training program is also a measurement problem

This is Anthropic trying to solve, at its own expense, a version of the exact gap Merit AC exists to measure: a company can buy Claude seats for every engineer it has, and that spend alone tells you nothing about whether any of those engineers can actually ship a governed, production-grade deployment with it. Treating that as a credentialing problem -- graded practicals, a residency, a named badge -- is Anthropic's bet that the skill gap, not the tool, is the bottleneck on enterprise AI value. Whether a "Claude Resident Engineer" badge predicts better outcomes than an ungraded Claude seat is exactly the kind of claim that needs real usage and outcome data to check, not just the credential itself.
