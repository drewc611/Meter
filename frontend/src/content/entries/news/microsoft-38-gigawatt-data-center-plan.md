Microsoft is planning to grow its global compute footprint from roughly 12 gigawatts today to more than 38 gigawatts by 2032, according to Bloomberg reporting carried on the Reuters wire on September 10, 2026, citing people familiar with the matter (Microsoft declined to comment). AI-dedicated capacity grows from about 2 gigawatts now to roughly a third of the 38-gigawatt total. Reported drivers include capacity shortages that forced Microsoft to turn away cloud and AI clients -- one retailer reportedly went to Oracle instead -- and an August outage tied to server constraints that took GitHub down for eight hours.

## The detail that changes how the number should be read

Microsoft is reportedly shifting new data-center leases from 15-year to 25-year terms, which lowers how much of the build cost gets recognized as capex in any given year. That's a real accounting consequence layered on top of the capacity story: the 38-gigawatt figure represents genuine physical buildout, but the pace at which it shows up on Microsoft's own financial statements is itself being actively managed.

## Turning away paying customers is the real signal

A hyperscaler declining business because it physically can't serve it -- not because of pricing or strategy -- is a more concrete data point on the AI infrastructure capacity crunch than any capex guidance number on its own. Microsoft's own FY2027 Q1 capex guidance sits at $50 billion, with roughly $175 billion projected for calendar 2026; for finance leaders modeling AI infrastructure costs, the turned-away-customer detail is the part worth weighing most heavily, since it's evidence of real constrained supply, not projected demand.
