Anthropic disclosed on September 23 that its newly formed life sciences research group -- established this spring to test whether general AI models can systematize biological discovery -- had Claude autonomously identify a previously uncharacterized enzyme system in bacteriophage DNA. Anthropic is calling it an array-associated reverse transcriptase (ART) system: a reverse transcriptase enzyme, a partner gene beside it, and a long array of evenly spaced DNA repeat sequences whose layout resembles a CRISPR array.

## The scale of the search, and the honest caveat

Claude reached this result after autonomously searching a database of roughly 1.9 billion protein clusters over a 21.5-hour campaign spanning 949 agent sessions and 215.6 million tokens -- a genuinely large-scale autonomous research run. But Anthropic is explicit about two limits: it doesn't yet know what ART does, and when the same search was repeated ten additional times, none of the follow-up runs reproduced the find. That reproducibility gap is the load-bearing fact here, not a footnote -- a single successful run against a near-2-billion-entry search space is a promising signal, not a confirmed discovery, and Anthropic is presenting it as exactly that.

## Why it matters beyond the specific finding

The result is Anthropic's first public evidence for the life sciences group's founding premise -- that a general-purpose model, not a narrow bio-specific one, can search unstructured biological data at a scale and speed no human team could match, and surface genuinely novel candidates. Whether ART turns out to be biologically significant is a separate, still-open question; what's demonstrated so far is the search capability, not yet a validated discovery.
