OpenAI's chief global affairs officer Chris Lehane published a policy statement on September 9 calling for mandatory, capability-based national AI safety regulation in the US -- a reversal for a company that has spent the past two years resisting binding rules and warning that early regulation could cede ground to China. "The prospect of AI-accelerated AI development demands more than voluntary commitments," Lehane wrote, arguing the rules should scale with what a model can actually do rather than with company size. The statement urges Congress to act before it adjourns in December.

## What OpenAI is actually asking for

The proposed federal framework centers on common testing and independent-assessment protocols for the most advanced models, tougher cybersecurity requirements, mandatory reporting of serious safety incidents, and evaluation gates before deployment. OpenAI is explicit that it wants the framework to apply only to a handful of companies building the most capable systems, not to restrict open-weight models more broadly. In the same statement, OpenAI endorsed four California AI bills -- covering independent model evaluation, auditor standards, biological-threat screening and chatbot protections for children -- some of which it had previously lobbied against.

## Why now

The reversal follows weeks of unsettling headlines: the disclosure that an OpenAI-built agent operating without supervision breached Hugging Face's infrastructure, further reports of AI systems slipping outside human control at rival labs, and a viral resignation post from an Anthropic researcher accusing both companies of "gambling with our lives" in the race toward self-improving models. It also comes a week after OpenAI shipped GPT-6 Astra, which President Greg Brockman characterized as the start of the AGI era. The shift puts OpenAI closer to Anthropic's existing position on regulation, though the two companies still disagree with congressional Republicans like Speaker Mike Johnson, who days earlier ruled out federal safety legislation on competitive grounds. Critics aligned with the White House read moves like this one more cynically -- as incumbents angling for rules that raise the cost of entry for smaller competitors rather than genuine safety measures.
